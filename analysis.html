Analysis · Swiss reimbursement

What the Spezialitätenliste reveals about how Switzerland pays for medicine

Every medicine Swiss mandatory health insurance covers sits on one public list, with its price attached. I analysed all 10,405 entries. Three patterns stood out — and one thing I expected to find turned out not to be there.

Abigail Anim · Data: BAG/FOPH Spezialitätenliste, extract of 13 August 2026 · 10,405 products, 10,253 with a listed price

Switzerland does something unusual: it publishes the entire reimbursement catalogue. Every product covered by the KVG, its public price, its patient co-payment tier, and the conditions attached to it are all openly available. Most countries treat at least some of this as commercially confidential.

That openness makes the list a rare object — a complete, machine-readable record of a national payer's decisions. I parsed the August 2026 extract to see what those decisions look like in aggregate.

1. There is no typical price

The median product on the list costs CHF 48.90. The mean costs CHF 1,707. When the mean is thirty-five times the median, the average has stopped describing anything real.

Seven in ten products cost less than CHF 100. At the other end sits Hemgenix, a haemophilia gene therapy, at CHF 2,757,269 per pack — roughly 56,000 times the median. The top 1% of products account for 84.7% of the summed list price of the entire catalogue.

The list is really two lists: a large, cheap, competitive tail of everyday medicines, and a small head of very expensive specialist products where almost all of the money sits.

This matters for how you read any statistic about Swiss drug prices. A policy that moves the median affects thousands of products and very little money. A policy that moves the head affects a handful of products and a great deal of money.

2. Price and conditions travel together

Roughly 41% of products on the list carry a Limitation — a formal condition restricting who can be prescribed it, for what indication, or in what quantity. These are the payer's main instrument for controlling use without refusing coverage outright.

Limitations are not spread evenly. Grouping products by the first letter of their ATC code and comparing each class's median price against the share of its products that carry a limitation shows a clear relationship.

Antineoplastic and immunomodulating agents (L) have a median price of CHF 428 and 60% carry limitations. Blood products (B) reach 68%. At the other end, sensory organ preparations (S) have a median of CHF 27.60 and only 10% are limited.

The correlation is moderate and the sample is fourteen classes, so this is a pattern rather than a proof. But it is consistent with what you would expect from a payer allocating its scrutiny where the money is.

3. Generic competition is patchy

Just over 45% of products on the list are flagged as generics. That headline number conceals a wide spread.

Cardiovascular medicines (C) are 63% generic — a mature, off-patent, heavily contested area. Hormonal preparations (H) are 16%, and the miscellaneous class (V), which contains many specialist and diagnostic products, is 17%.

Where generics do exist, the price gap is substantial.264 products on the list have a cheaper generic alternative in the same substance class and the same pack size. The median saving is 10.6% of the original's price, on 264 like-for-like pairs where the pack size matches

Switzerland pushes patients toward those alternatives with a blunt instrument: the differential co-payment. Standard patient co-insurance is 10%, but 385 products carry a 40% rate, applied where a cheaper equivalent exists and the patient chooses the original anyway. That is only 3.7% of the list — a narrowly targeted penalty rather than a broad one.

What I expected to find, and didn't

I assumed generic penetration would track price: that expensive classes would attract more generic entry because the commercial prize is larger. It doesn't. The correlation between a class's median price and its generic share is ρ = −0.19 with p = 0.51 — statistically indistinguishable from no relationship.

On reflection this makes sense. Generic entry is governed by patent expiry and manufacturing difficulty, not by price. The most expensive classes are expensive precisely because they are still on patent or are biologics that are hard to copy. Price is a symptom of the same thing that blocks generic entry, not a driver of it.

It's the sort of hypothesis that sounds obvious until you test it, which is why it's worth reporting the null result rather than quietly dropping it.

What this analysis cannot tell you. The Spezialitätenliste records prices, not volumes. Nothing here says how much Switzerland actually spends — a CHF 2.76 million therapy given to four patients costs the system less than a CHF 12 painkiller dispensed to millions. Where I refer to the "summed list price of the catalogue", that is a statement about the distribution of prices across products, not about expenditure.

Prices are public prices (Publikumspreis) including VAT, not ex-factory prices, and exclude confidential rebates negotiated between BAG and manufacturers. Therapeutic classes are grouped by first-level ATC code, which is coarse. The extract is a snapshot from 13 August 2026; the list is revised monthly.

Method

The August 2026 Datenstamm and Limitierungen files were downloaded from the BAG electronic services platform as Excel, parsed in Python with openpyxl, and analysed with pandas and scipy. Pack sizes were extracted from free-text package descriptions with a regular-expression parser that multiplies all quantities in the string — so "5 Fertspr 3 ml" resolves correctly to 15 ml — and succeeded on 10,403 of 10,405 records. Generic alternatives were matched within ATC code and pack size, taking the lowest-priced product flagged as a generic. An earlier version of this analysis matched on ATC code alone, which compared packs of different sizes and overstated the median saving; that figure has been corrected. Correlations are Spearman rank, appropriate here because the price distribution is heavily skewed.

The same cleaned dataset powers SLCheck, a lookup tool I built for the same list.
